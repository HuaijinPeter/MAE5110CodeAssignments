# Model

Stance is an inverted pendulum with an ankle torque,

$$\ddot{\theta} = \frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta + \frac{\tau}{m\ell^2},$$

with $\theta$ clockwise from the upward vertical. The swing leg is held at a
half-angle $\alpha$, so touchdown happens on the guard
$\theta_{\text{TD}} = \alpha + \gamma$, and the collision relabels the legs:

$$\theta^+ = \theta^- - 2\alpha, \qquad \dot{\theta}^+ = \cos(2\alpha)\,\dot{\theta}^-.$$

Parameters are $g = 9.81$ m/s$^2$, $\ell = 1$ m, $m = 1$ kg, $\gamma = 0.06$ rad,
and the inputs are bounded by

$$\alpha \in \left[\tfrac{\pi}{8}, \tfrac{\pi}{7}\right] = [0.3927,\ 0.4488]\ \text{rad}, \qquad \tau \in [-0.1\,mg\ell,\ 0.05\,mg\ell] = [-0.981,\ 0.4905]\ \text{N\,m}.$$

As instructed, `dynamics(t, state, params)` keeps its signature and
`ankle_torque` lives in the parameter dictionary, so the choice of control input
can change without touching the model.

# Standing controller

Feedback linearization cancels gravity and imposes a linear second-order system,

$$\tau = -mg\ell\sin\theta + m\ell^2\left(-k_p\theta - k_d\dot{\theta}\right),
\qquad k_p = 6,\ k_d = 3,$$

saturated to the admissible range. Unsaturated this gives
$\ddot{\theta} = -k_p\theta - k_d\dot{\theta}$, i.e. $\omega_n = 2.449$ rad/s and
$\zeta = 0.612$. `test_walker.py` checks the cancellation algebraically and
checks that the commanded torque never leaves the bounds.

![**Standing controller.** From $\theta_0 = 0.02$ rad, converging to
$|\theta|, |\dot\theta| \approx 10^{-5}$. The commanded torque stays inside
$[-0.316,\ 0.032]$ N m, well within the limits.
](output/assignment_2/standing_controller.png)

## Region of attraction

The RoA is gridded over the stance angles the walker can reach,
$\theta \in [\gamma - \alpha,\ \gamma + \alpha] = [-0.333,\ 0.453]$ rad, and
$\dot{\theta} \in [-1.5,\ 1.5]$ rad/s. A state counts as caught if the closed
loop stays within $10^{-2}$ of the origin over the last 0.2 s of a 5 s
simulation. The script confirms the RoA does not touch the velocity edges of the
window.

![**Region of attraction.** 203 of 2501 gridded states (8.1%).
](output/assignment_2/standing_roa.png)

The RoA is a narrow diagonal band rather than a ball: what the controller can
absorb is an energy-like quantity, so leaning forward is survivable while moving
backwards. The band is thin because of saturation, not gain tuning. Holding a
fixed angle needs $\tau = -mg\ell\sin\theta$, so the statically holdable band is

$$\theta \in [-\arcsin 0.05,\ \arcsin 0.10] = [-0.050,\ 0.100]\ \text{rad},$$

asymmetric because the torque limits are. Outside it no admissible torque holds
the walker up, so all the incoming kinetic energy must go within about 0.1 rad of
travel. Raising $k_p$ and $k_d$ does not help --- the demand saturates
immediately.

What matters downstream is where the RoA cuts the section. Bisection
(`compute_roa_velocity_bound`, tolerance $10^{-3}$) gives
$\dot{\theta} \in [-0.156,\ +0.313]$ rad/s at $\theta = 0$. The walker always
arrives moving forwards, so the operative number is
$\dot{\theta}_{\text{RoA}} = 0.3127$ rad/s, and that comparison is the event
guard: the torque stays off until the state enters the RoA.

# Choice of Poincaré section

**Section: $\theta = 0$, crossed with $\dot{\theta} > 0$.** Three reasons:

1. **It does not move with the control input.** The touchdown surface
   $\theta = \alpha + \gamma$ worked for the rimless wheel because geometry
   pinned $\alpha$. Once $\alpha$ is an input, that surface sweeps across state
   space whenever the controller changes its mind.
2. **It is transverse to the relevant flow.** Any walking orbit has
   $\dot{\theta} > 0$ at mid-stance, so the flow crosses $\theta = 0$
   transversally. Transversality fails only for orbits that stall at the
   vertical, which are the failures we want to detect anyway.
3. **It makes $\dot{\theta}_k$ the only state**, since $\theta \equiv 0$ there,
   giving $\dot{\theta}_{k+1} = f(\dot{\theta}_k, \alpha_k)$.

It is also the surface on which the RoA is a single interval
$[0,\ \dot\theta_{\text{RoA}}]$, so "have I arrived?" is one comparison. One
application of the map runs mid-stance $\to$ touchdown $\to$ impact $\to$ next
mid-stance (`integrate_step_to_next_crossing`).

![**Return map** for three values of $\alpha$. Every curve lies below the
identity: on a slope this shallow, walking always loses speed. Larger $\alpha$
means a longer step, a more oblique collision and more energy lost, and it also
raises the speed below which a step no longer completes --- where each curve
terminates. Note the near-vertical tangent at each left end: a small change in
incoming speed produces a large change in outgoing speed.
](output/assignment_2/poincare_map.png)

Because stance is conservative the map has a closed form,

$$\dot{\theta}_{k+1}^2 =
\cos^2(2\alpha)\left[\dot{\theta}_k^2 + \tfrac{2g}{\ell}\bigl(1 - \cos(\alpha+\gamma)\bigr)\right]
- \tfrac{2g}{\ell}\bigl(1 - \cos(\alpha-\gamma)\bigr),$$

which `test_walker.py` uses as an oracle. The integrated map agrees with it to
$2.4\times10^{-3}$ rad/s across the whole range, so integration error is not what
limits the table. Two features drive everything below:

- **A stall threshold.** A step completes only if the post-impact state can climb
  back to the vertical: minimum entry speed $0.418$ rad/s for $\alpha_{\min}$,
  $1.131$ rad/s for $\alpha_{\max}$.
- **A dead band.** Velocities in $(0.313,\ 0.418)$ rad/s are too fast to catch
  and too slow to step. Nothing recovers from there, so any plan whose last step
  lands in that band fails, however good it looks on the grid.

# Look-up table and grid resolution

The state axis sweeps $\dot{\theta}_k \in [0,\ \sqrt{2g/\ell}] = [0,\ 4.429]$
rad/s (Froude 2); the action axis sweeps $\alpha$ in 31 points.

![**State--action table.** White cells never reach the next crossing; the wedge
at the top left is the stall region, where slow states can only afford small
$\alpha$.](output/assignment_2/transition_table.png)

The policy is a backwards search: cells inside the RoA are 0 steps, and a state
is $n+1$ steps as soon as some $\alpha$ sends it to a state already labelled $n$.

## Why the look-up rounds up

$\dot{\theta}_{k+1}$ almost never lands on a grid point, so the table needs a
rounding rule. Rounding to the **nearest** point --- the obvious choice --- turned
out to be unsound rather than merely imprecise. Differentiating the closed form,

$$\frac{\partial \dot{\theta}_{k+1}}{\partial \dot{\theta}_k}
= \cos^2(2\alpha)\,\frac{\dot{\theta}_k}{\dot{\theta}_{k+1}},$$

which blows up as $\dot{\theta}_{k+1} \to 0$ --- exactly where a minimum-step
policy wants to be, since landing near zero is how you reach the RoA in one step.
A greedy policy therefore picks the most sensitive actions available, and half a
cell of look-up error is amplified into a landing in the dead band. There is a
second failure mode: the tabulated state can be *faster* than the true one, so a
step the table calls feasible may stall.

The fix is to treat each grid point as the **upper edge** of its cell and round
up (`look_up_state_index`), and to require a planned step to complete from the
cell's slowest state (`is_step_feasible_over_cell`). Since both the return map and
the cost-to-go increase with speed, the tabulated state is never slower than the
true one, so by induction the true state stays at or below the state the plan
tracks. The table becomes a guaranteed upper bound: the walker reaches the RoA in
*at most* the tabulated number of steps. Coarse grids are then pessimistic rather
than wrong, and refinement becomes monotone.

## Verifying the resolution

**Criterion.** A grid is fine enough when, for every off-grid test velocity, its
policy brings the walker into the RoA *on the true continuous dynamics* --- the
un-snapped velocity carried forward, the grid used only to look up the next
action --- and the tabulated step count equals the steps actually taken.

This is a closed-loop test, not a discretisation bound. An interpolation bound
would only say $\dot{\theta}_{k+1}$ is represented accurately; the sensitivity
above is exactly why that is not the same as the policy working. The test set is
59 cell-centred velocities spanning $[0,\ 4.429]$ rad/s, off-grid for every
candidate, filtered to states either already in the RoA or able to take one step,
so a failure can only be blamed on the grid.

**Result: 49 points, spacing 0.0923 rad/s** --- the coarsest grid that passes, and
the value `GRID_VELOCITY_POINTS` is set to. One notch coarser, at **47 points**
and spacing 0.0963 rad/s, the test state $\dot\theta_0 = 0.472$ rad/s gets no
plan at all.

The mechanism is not generic round-off. That state sits just above the stall
threshold of 0.418 rad/s. At 47 points it lands in the cell $(0.3852,\ 0.4815]$,
whose lower edge is below the threshold, so the cell holds states that cannot step
and the planner refuses it. At 49 points the same state lands in
$(0.4614,\ 0.5537]$, whose lower edge clears the threshold. The requirement is
therefore set by how finely the grid resolves the stall threshold, not by
interpolation accuracy.

The nearest-point columns are the control. They never pass, and they do not
improve with resolution: 20, 22, 27, 22, 24, 41, 41, 36, 23, 45, 27, 47 failures
from 11 to 101 points --- a 101-point grid is worse than an 11-point one. Whether
a grid happens to work depends on where its points fall relative to
$\dot\theta_{\text{RoA}}$ and to the stall threshold, which is arbitrary. That is
an unsound rounding rule, not an under-resolved grid.

## A trajectory needing at least three steps

Among states needing at least 3 steps, the script takes the one needing the most.
On the 49-point grid that is $\dot\theta_0 = 3.5066$ rad/s, labelled **4 steps
minimum** and **6 steps maximum**.

Both policies run **in closed loop on the true dynamics**: the action is looked up
from the velocity the simulation produces at each crossing, walking stops when the
true state enters the RoA, and the ankle controller takes over. This matters ---
replaying a precomputed action sequence open-loop drifts away from the states
those actions were chosen for, and the maximum-step plan then stalls, since it
deliberately hugs the slow edge of the feasible set.

- **Minimum, 4 steps.** Mid-stance velocities 3.5066, 2.4619, 1.7153, 1.0735,
  0.2249 rad/s, with $\alpha$ = 0.3927, 0.3927, 0.4114, 0.4394 rad.
- **Maximum, 6 steps.** Mid-stance velocities 3.5066, 2.4619, 1.7153, 1.1769,
  0.7782, 0.4648, 0.1435 rad/s, with $\alpha = \alpha_{\min}$ throughout.

Both end at rest, $|\theta|, |\dot\theta| \approx 10^{-5}$, so the tabulated
counts of 4 and 6 are what the simulation delivers.

![**Step-to-step trajectories.** The first two steps are identical: at high speed
even $\alpha_{\min}$ sheds plenty, so there is nothing to choose between the

# What I would change next

- `compute_minimum_step_policy` and `compute_maximum_step_policy` share their
  structure apart from the objective and should be one search parameterised by a
  comparison. I kept them apart because the maximum-step version also has to break
  cycles, which is not a good enough reason.
- Explicit Euler at $10^{-4}$ s is wasteful for a conservative system. RK4 at a
  larger step, or the closed form already used as a test oracle, would turn the
  grid study from minutes into seconds.
- The action grid was never swept the way the state grid was. Since the policy
  leans on actions near the stall boundary, $\alpha$ resolution probably matters
  as much, and the same closed-loop criterion would answer it.
