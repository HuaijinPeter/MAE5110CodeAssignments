## 1. Model

The stance phase is an inverted pendulum with a torque source at the ankle,

$$\ddot{\theta} = \frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta + \frac{\tau}{m\ell^2},$$

with $\theta$ measured clockwise from the upward vertical. The swing leg is held
at a fixed half-angle $\alpha$ from the stance leg, so touchdown happens on the
guard $\theta_{\mathrm{TD}} = \alpha + \gamma$, and the collision re-labels the
legs and scales the angular velocity by the cosine of the inter-leg angle:

$$\theta^+ = \theta^- - 2\alpha, \qquad \dot{\theta}^+ = \cos(2\alpha)\,\dot{\theta}^-.$$

Parameters: $g = 9.81~\mathrm{m/s^2}$, $\ell = 1~\mathrm{m}$, $m = 1~\mathrm{kg}$,
$\gamma = 0.06~\mathrm{rad}$. The two control inputs are bounded by

$$\alpha \in \left[\frac{\pi}{8}, \frac{\pi}{7}\right] = [0.3927,\ 0.4488]~\mathrm{rad},
\qquad
\tau \in \left[-0.1\,mg\ell,\ 0.05\,mg\ell\right] = [-0.981,\ 0.4905]~\mathrm{N\,m}.$$

As instructed, `dynamics(t, state, params)` keeps its signature and
`ankle_torque` lives in the parameter dictionary, defaulting to zero, so which
quantity plays the role of a control input can change without touching the
model. Both controllers live in the experiment script, at the same level as the
integration loop.

## 3. Standing controller: feedback linearization

The ankle torque cancels gravity and imposes a linear second-order system,

$$\tau = -mg\ell\sin\theta + m\ell^2\left(-k_p\theta - k_d\dot{\theta}\right),
\qquad k_p = 6,\ k_d = 3,$$

saturated to the admissible torque range. Unsaturated, the closed loop is
$\ddot{\theta} = -k_p\theta - k_d\dot{\theta}$, i.e. $\omega_n = 2.449$ rad/s
and $\zeta = 0.612$. `test_walker.py` checks the cancellation algebraically and
checks that the commanded torque never leaves the admissible range.

![Figure 3](output/assignment_2/standing_controller.png)

**Figure 3 — standing controller.** Response from $\theta_0 = 0.02$ rad,
converging to $|\theta|, |\dot\theta| \approx 10^{-5}$. The commanded torque
stays within $[-0.316,\ 0.032]$ N m here, well inside the limits, because the
initial state is deep inside the RoA.

### Region of attraction

The RoA is found by gridding initial states over the stance-angle range the
walker can physically be in, $\theta \in [\gamma - \alpha,\ \gamma + \alpha] =
[-0.333,\ 0.453]$ rad (post-impact angle to touchdown angle) and
$\dot{\theta} \in [-1.5,\ 1.5]$ rad/s, then simulating the closed loop for 5 s
and requiring the state to stay within $10^{-2}$ of the origin over the last
0.2 s. The script checks and reports that the RoA does not touch the velocity
edges of the search window, so the window is wide enough.

![Figure 4](output/assignment_2/standing_roa.png)

**Figure 4 — region of attraction.** 203 of 2501 gridded initial states (8.1%).

The RoA is a narrow *diagonal band*, not a ball around the origin: what the
controller can absorb is an energy-like quantity, so leaning forward is
survivable if you are already moving backwards. The band is thin because of
torque saturation rather than gain tuning. Holding the pendulum at a fixed angle
requires $\tau = -mg\ell\sin\theta$, so the statically holdable band is

$$\theta \in [-\arcsin 0.05,\ \arcsin 0.10] = [-0.050,\ 0.100]~\mathrm{rad},$$

and it is asymmetric because the torque limits are. Outside that band no
admissible torque holds the walker up, so all the incoming kinetic energy has to
be removed within about 0.1 rad of travel. Raising $k_p$ and $k_d$ does not
help: the demanded torque saturates immediately.

**The number that matters downstream** is where the RoA cuts the Poincaré
section. Bisection (`compute_roa_velocity_bound`, tolerance $10^{-3}$) gives

$$\dot{\theta} \in [-0.156,\ +0.313]~\mathrm{rad/s} \quad \mathrm{at}\ \theta = 0.$$

The walker always arrives at mid-stance moving forwards, so the operative number
is $\dot{\theta}_{\mathrm{RoA}} = 0.3127$ rad/s. The event guard that switches the
ankle controller on is exactly this test: the torque stays at zero until the
state enters the RoA, then feedback linearization takes over and the walk ends.

## 4. Choice of Poincaré section

**Section: $\theta = 0$ (mid-stance, stance leg vertical), crossed with
$\dot{\theta} > 0$.**

Three reasons, all visible in Figure 2:

1. **It does not move with the control input.** The touchdown surface
   $\theta = \alpha + \gamma$ was a good section for the rimless wheel because
   geometry pinned $\alpha$ to the spoke count. Once $\alpha$ is an input, that
   surface sweeps across state space whenever the controller changes its mind,
   and the state recorded on it stops being comparable between steps.
   $\theta = 0$ is fixed by the model, not by the policy.
2. **It is transverse to the relevant flow.** On any orbit that is actually
   walking, $\dot{\theta} > 0$ at mid-stance, so the vector field has a strictly
   positive component normal to $\theta = 0$. Transversality only fails for
   orbits that stall at the vertical — which are precisely the failures we want
   to detect anyway.
3. **It makes $\dot{\theta}_k$ the only state.** $\theta \equiv 0$ on the
   section, so the return map is one-dimensional,
   $\dot{\theta}_{k+1} = f(\dot{\theta}_k, \alpha_k)$, with a single input.

As a bonus, this is also the surface on which the standing controller's RoA is a
single interval $[0,\ \dot\theta_{\mathrm{RoA}}]$, so "have I arrived?" is one
scalar comparison.

One application of the map therefore runs mid-stance → touchdown → impact →
next mid-stance (`integrate_step_to_next_crossing`).

![Figure 5](output/assignment_2/poincare_map.png)

**Figure 5 — the return map** for three values of $\alpha$. Every curve lies
below the identity line: on a slope this shallow, walking always loses speed, so
the step-to-step dynamics are a controlled descent to standstill. Larger
$\alpha$ means a longer step, a more oblique collision, and more energy lost —
and it also raises the speed below which a step no longer completes, which is
where each curve terminates. Note the near-vertical tangent at the left end of
every curve: a tiny change in the incoming speed there produces a large change
in the outgoing speed. That is the sensitivity that Section 5 has to defend
against, and it is visible here before any grid is involved.

### Structure of the step-to-step dynamics

Because the stance phase is conservative, the return map has a closed form,

$$\dot{\theta}_{k+1}^2 =
\cos^2(2\alpha)\left[\dot{\theta}_k^2 + \frac{2g}{\ell}(1 - \cos(\alpha+\gamma))\right]
- \frac{2g}{\ell}(1 - \cos(\alpha-\gamma)),$$

which `test_walker.py` uses as an oracle for the simulated map. Over the whole
state and action range the integrated map agrees with the exact solution to
within $2.4\times10^{-3}$ rad/s — more than an order of magnitude below any grid
spacing considered below, so integration error is not what limits the look-up
table.

The closed form also exposes the two features that drive everything in Section 5:

- **A stall threshold.** A step only completes if the post-impact state has the
  energy to climb back to the vertical. The minimum entry speed is $0.418$ rad/s
  for $\alpha_{\min}$ and $1.131$ rad/s for $\alpha_{\max}$; below it the walker
  rolls back.
- **A dead band.** Velocities in $(0.313,\ 0.418)$ rad/s are too fast for the
  ankle controller to catch and too slow to take another step. Nothing recovers
  from there. Any plan whose last step lands in that band fails, however good it
  looks on the grid.

## 5. Look-up table, rounding rule, and grid resolution

The state axis sweeps $\dot{\theta}_k \in [0,\ \sqrt{2g/\ell}] = [0,\ 4.429]$
rad/s (Froude number 2); the action axis sweeps $\alpha \in [\pi/8,\ \pi/7]$ in
31 points.

![Figure 6](output/assignment_2/transition_table.png)

**Figure 6 — the state–action table.** $\dot{\theta}_{k+1}$ as a function of
state and action. White cells are pairs for which the walker never reaches the
next crossing; the white wedge at the top left is the stall region, where slow
states can only afford small $\alpha$.

The policy is a backwards breadth-first search over this table: states whose
cell lies inside the RoA are labelled 0 steps, and a state is labelled $n+1$
steps as soon as some admissible $\alpha$ sends it into a state already labelled
$n$.

### Why the look-up rounds up instead of to the nearest point

$\dot{\theta}_{k+1}$ almost never lands on a grid point, so the table needs a
rounding rule. Rounding to the **closest** grid point — the obvious choice — was
the single largest source of error in this assignment, and it is worth being
explicit about why.

Differentiating the closed-form map,

$$\frac{\partial \dot{\theta}_{k+1}}{\partial \dot{\theta}_k}
= \cos^2(2\alpha)\,\frac{\dot{\theta}_k}{\dot{\theta}_{k+1}},$$

which blows up as $\dot{\theta}_{k+1} \to 0$ — exactly the regime a
minimum-step policy wants to be in, since landing near zero is how you get
inside the RoA in one step. A greedy policy therefore systematically picks the
most sensitive actions available, and a half-cell look-up error is amplified
into a landing that misses the RoA and drops into the dead band. There is also a
second failure mode with nearest-point rounding: the tabulated state can be
*faster* than the true one, so a step the table says is feasible may stall.

The fix is to treat each grid point as the **upper edge** of its cell and round
look-ups up (`look_up_state_index`), plus to require that a planned step also
completes from the slowest state the cell can hold
(`is_step_feasible_over_cell`). Because both the return map and the cost-to-go
increase with speed, the tabulated state is then never slower than the true one,
so by induction the true state stays at or below the state the plan is tracking.
The table becomes a guaranteed upper bound: the walker reaches the RoA in *at
most* the tabulated number of steps. Coarse grids become merely pessimistic,
never wrong — and refinement becomes monotone, which is what makes the study
below meaningful.

### Verifying the grid resolution

**Criterion.** A grid is fine enough when, for every off-grid test velocity, the
policy read out of that grid brings the walker into the RoA *when executed on
the true continuous dynamics* — with the un-snapped velocity carried forward and
the grid used only to look up the next action — and the tabulated step count
equals the number of steps actually taken.

This is a closed-loop test rather than a discretisation-error bound. An
interpolation bound would only say that $\dot{\theta}_{k+1}$ is *represented*
accurately; it would say nothing about whether the resulting *policy* works,
which is the property actually needed. The sensitivity argument above is exactly
why the two are not the same here.

The test set is 59 cell-centred velocities spanning $[0,\ 4.429]$ rad/s —
deliberately off-grid for every candidate grid — filtered to states that are
either already in the RoA or can complete at least one step, so a failure can
only be blamed on the grid.

![Figure 7](output/assignment_2/grid_resolution.png)

**Figure 7 — grid resolution study**, both rounding rules.

**Result: 49 points, spacing 0.0923 rad/s.** This is the coarsest grid that
passes, and it is the value `GRID_VELOCITY_POINTS` is set to; every figure in
Section 6 uses it. The margin is tight, which is the point: at **47 points**,
one notch coarser at spacing 0.0963 rad/s — a 4% change — the test state
$\dot\theta_0 = 0.472$ rad/s is left without a plan at all and never reaches
the RoA.

The mechanism is worth spelling out, because it is not generic round-off. That
test state sits just above the stall threshold of 0.418 rad/s. With 47 points
it falls in the cell whose upper edge is $v = 0.4815$ and whose lower edge is
$v = 0.3852$ — and 0.3852 is *below* the stall threshold, so the cell contains
states that cannot take a step at all and the planner correctly refuses it.
With 49 points the same test state falls in the cell $(0.4614,\ 0.5537]$, whose
lower edge clears the threshold, so the cell is planned and the state is
solved. The resolution requirement here is therefore set by how finely the grid
has to resolve the stall threshold, not by interpolation accuracy. Everything
finer than 49 also passes with zero step-count error, which is what makes 49 a
floor rather than a lucky draw.

The nearest-point columns are the control, and they are the reason the rounding
rule needed changing at all. They never pass, and — more tellingly — they do
not improve with resolution: 20, 22, 27, 22, 24, 41, 41, 36, 23, 45, 27, 47
failures as the grid goes from 11 to 101 points. A 101-point grid is *worse*
than an 11-point one. Whether a particular grid happens to work depends on
where its points land relative to $\dot\theta_{\mathrm{RoA}} = 0.3127$ and
relative to the stall threshold, which is essentially arbitrary. That is the
signature of an unsound rounding rule rather than an under-resolved grid, and
no amount of refinement fixes it.

![Figure 9](output/assignment_2/minimum_step_policy.png)

**Figure 9 — the policy.** The sawtooth is the interesting part. Within a band
of constant step count, $\alpha$ ramps up with velocity: the faster you arrive,
the longer a step you take, so that the landing still falls inside the *same*
lower band. At each band edge one more step becomes unavoidable, $\alpha$ drops
back to $\alpha_{\min}$, and the ramp starts again.

### A trajectory that needs at least three steps

The script picks the initial condition automatically: among the states that
need at least 3 steps, it takes the one that needs the most. On the verified
49-point grid that is $\dot\theta_0 = 3.5066$ rad/s, which the table labels
**4 steps minimum** and **6 steps maximum**.

Both policies are then executed **in closed loop on the true continuous
dynamics** — the action is looked up from the velocity the simulation actually
produces at each mid-stance crossing, walking stops as soon as the true state
enters the RoA, and the ankle controller takes over from there. This matters:
replaying a pre-computed action sequence open-loop drifts away from the states
those actions were chosen for, and the maximum-step plan in particular then
stalls, because it deliberately hugs the slow edge of the feasible set.

| | steps | mid-stance velocities $\dot\theta_k$ (rad/s) | actions $\alpha_k$ (rad) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minimum | 4 | 3.5066, 2.4619, 1.7153, 1.0735, 0.2249 | 0.3927, 0.3927, 0.4114, 0.4394 |
| Maximum | 6 | 3.5066, 2.4619, 1.7153, 1.1769, 0.7782, 0.4648, 0.1435 | 0.3927 (all six) |

Both runs end at rest: the final state is $|\theta|, |\dot\theta| \approx
10^{-5}$ in each case, and the tabulated step counts, 4 and 6, are exactly what
the continuous simulation delivers.

![Figure 10](output/assignment_2/step_trajectories.png)

**Figure 10 — the two step-to-step trajectories.** The first two steps are
identical: at high speed even $\alpha_{\min}$ is more than enough, so there is
nothing to choose between the policies yet. They separate at crossing 3. The

![Figure 11](output/assignment_2/continuous_trajectories.png)

**Figure 11 — the same two runs in state space.** Each arc rises from

## 7. What I would change next

- `compute_minimum_step_policy` and `compute_maximum_step_policy` share their
  entire structure apart from the objective; they should be one search
  parameterised by a comparison. I left them apart because the maximum-step
  version also has to break cycles, but that is not a good enough reason.
- Explicit Euler at $10^{-4}$ s is wasteful for a conservative system. RK4 at a
  much larger step — or the closed-form map of Section 4, which is already in
  the test suite as an oracle — would turn the grid study from minutes into
  seconds.
- The action grid was never swept the way the state grid was. Since the policy
  leans on actions near the stall boundary, the $\alpha$ resolution probably
  matters as much as the velocity resolution, and the same closed-loop criterion
  would answer it.
